# Three readings, not three required repairs

**Newly authored editorial illustrations.** The passages, names, contexts and annotations below are original to this edition. They are not drawn from actual manuscripts or critique sessions and were not processed by an evaluation system. Line labels are stable references for the discussion, not a machine input format.

## A. The satchel: a supported continuity problem

**Supplied context:** A realistic scene in continuous time. There is one blue satchel, no duplicate, no intervening retrieval and no change of viewpoint. The three lines are the complete action being reviewed.

```text
A1  The freight cage shuddered up past the second-floor grating,
    taking Ivo's blue satchel with it.
A2  Ivo stayed at the loading desk below.
A3  Without leaving the desk, he opened that same satchel and
    took out the delivery slip.
```

**Candidate reading:** The action contains a physical continuity break. A1 moves the satchel upstairs; A2 keeps Ivo below; A3 requires him to open it at the desk. The supplied context rules out a missing return or a second bag. This is more specific than calling the passage confusing or poorly paced.

**Effect and confidence:** The contradiction interrupts the reader's ability to reconstruct the action. Confidence is high within the supplied contract; its severity is local unless later events depend on the slip's custody. It is not evidence that the entire story is incoherent.

**Possible cause, not established fact:** A revision may have changed when the cage departed without updating the next action. Nothing in the excerpt establishes that editing history.

**Boundary check:** A time cut, a second satchel or an established impossible world could change the reading, but none is available in this example. Inventing one solely to excuse A3 would evade the actual draft problem.

**Optional revision choices:** Take the slip out before A1, preserving the scene's speed but making Ivo prepared. Alternatively, let him discover the mistake and retrieve the satchel, adding delay and potentially changing his characterization. The writer chooses which consequence belongs in this scene. Neither revision is automatically the best story.

## B. The second bowl: stillness worth keeping

**Supplied context:** A quiet standalone vignette. The author wants the reader to remain uncertain whether someone is expected, remembered or being refused farewell. No reveal is promised within this scene.

```text
B1  Lena set a second bowl opposite hers.
B2  When the steam thinned, she moved it nearer the stove.
B3  At midnight she ate from her own bowl and washed only that one.
```

**Plausible candidate label:** Not enough happens; explain the absent person's identity or add an arrival.

**Why that reading is rejected here:** B2 makes waiting an action: Lena preserves something for the absent place. B3 maintains the distinction between her bowl and the other one. The scene does not need a visitor to turn uncertainty into a meaningful experience. The proposed explanation would settle precisely what the author intends to leave open.

**Strength grounded in the text:** The domestic actions carry the emotional relation without naming it. B1 establishes the empty place, B2 sustains it, and B3 refuses to treat both bowls alike. Waiting, mourning and refusal remain readings, not certified backstory.

**Confidence and limits:** There is enough support to reject a mandatory repair on the grounds of inactivity. There is no measured claim that every reader will find the vignette moving, nor proof that a whole chapter of similar beats would work equally well.

**Decision:** Keep the ambiguity in this example. If the author later wants a different effect, ask what the reader should understand about the absence before proposing a reveal. Adding a name could make the relationship more specific while narrowing the range of interpretations; that is a creative tradeoff, not a correction owed to a checklist.

## C. The agreement: ask for the missing draft

**Supplied context:** An editor's margin note says the earlier refusal scene is being rewritten. Neither that scene nor its replacement is supplied. The following is a provisional continuation, not an approved account of the full character arc.

```text
C1  At the station, Corin touched the unsigned agreement in his pocket.
C2  "I'll come," he said, before the conductor finished asking.
```

**Candidate reading:** The change from refusal to agreement may be underprepared.

**Evidence and limitation:** C2 shows an expressed decision to come. The margin note refers to a changing earlier scene; it is not that scene's text and does not establish the current version of Corin's motive. C1 does not prove he signed, delivered or fulfilled the agreement.

**Disposition:** Unresolved because the relevant draft context is missing. Do not label the character inconsistent or add a retrospective explanation yet. The symptom is a possible gap between versions; its cause and severity remain unestablished.

**Question before revision:** Which refusal scene, if any, remains in the current draft, and what does the author intend the reader to infer between it and C2? Inspect that version before deciding whether a bridging beat is needed.

**Boundary check:** The visible margin note already marks revision work. It is not an unmarked canonical contradiction simply because the old and new possibilities differ. If the complete current draft supplies the turn, no local repair may be needed. If it does not, consider a small decision beat, weighing the added explanation against the present abruptness.

## What these examples demonstrate

They demonstrate an inspectable way to write a critique: evidence, competing readings, limits and a proportionate next step. They do not demonstrate automatic diagnosis or objective literary quality. A identifies a contradiction under a declared scene contract; B rejects an overbroad rule; C leaves judgment open until the right text is available. The same uncertainty label would conceal those differences.
